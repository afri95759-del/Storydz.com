🔫 The Assassination of Indira Gandhi (1984)

31 Bullets. Two Sikh Bodyguards. One Prime Minister.

October 31, 1984 — 9:20 AM New Delhi, India — 1 Safdarjung Road Reading time: 14 minutes Killed by her own protectors
Date
October 31, 1984
Location
New Delhi, India
Assassins
Beant Singh & Satwant Singh
Shots Fired
31 bullets

📑 Table of Contents

31

That's how many bullets tore through India's Prime Minister — fired by the very men sworn to protect her.

She trusted them. She refused to remove them from her security detail. They killed her.

On the morning of October 31, 1984, Indira Gandhi — the Prime Minister of India, the most powerful woman on Earth, and the daughter of India's founding father Jawaharlal Nehru — walked from her residence toward her office. She was 66 years old. Her face was lined with exhaustion from leading the world's largest democracy through relentless crises. She wore a simple cotton sari. She passed through her garden gate — a path she had walked thousands of times. Two of her personal security guards, both Sikhs, stood waiting. Beant Singh, a police sub-inspector who had served in Gandhi's security detail for 10 years, raised his .38 service revolver and fired three shots at point-blank range. Satwant Singh, a 21-year-old constable assigned to her detail just five months earlier, stepped forward with his Sten submachine gun and unleashed 25 rounds into her collapsing body. Indira Gandhi crumpled to the gravel path. The assassins dropped their weapons. "We have done what we had to do," Beant Singh said, raising his hands. "Now you do what you want." He was shot dead on the spot by other guards. Satwant Singh was captured alive. Indira Gandhi was rushed to the hospital. Doctors fought for five hours. She had been hit by 31 bullets. At 2:20 PM, she was declared dead. This was not just a political murder. This was revenge — cold, calculated, and catastrophic. It was the Sikh community's furious response to Operation Blue Star, the military assault on the Golden Temple that Gandhi had ordered five months earlier. And it would unleash a wave of anti-Sikh violence that would leave thousands dead and plunge India into one of its darkest chapters since Partition.

Summary: Indira Gandhi, the Prime Minister of India, was assassinated on October 31, 1984, by two of her Sikh bodyguards — Beant Singh and Satwant Singh — in the garden of her New Delhi residence. The assassination was a direct retaliation for Operation Blue Star (June 1-8, 1984), in which Gandhi ordered the Indian Army to storm the Golden Temple in Amritsar — the holiest site in Sikhism — to flush out Sikh militant leader Jarnail Singh Bhindranwale. The military operation killed over 500 people and caused massive damage to the sacred complex. Gandhi's assassination triggered anti-Sikh pogroms across northern India, in which an estimated 3,000-8,000 Sikhs were massacred over four days. Gandhi's son, Rajiv Gandhi, succeeded her as Prime Minister. Beant Singh was killed at the scene; Satwant Singh was hanged in 1989.

👑 The Iron Lady of India: Who Was Indira Gandhi?

Indira Gandhi was born into India's political aristocracy in 1917 — the only child of Jawaharlal Nehru, India's first Prime Minister. She was not raised in a traditional home but in the crucible of nationalist politics, amid civil disobedience, British jail sentences, and the towering presence of Mahatma Gandhi (no relation). When she became Prime Minister in 1966, the Congress Party bosses expected a docile figurehead they could control. They were spectacularly wrong. Indira Gandhi proved to be a ruthless and authoritarian leader. She centralized power in her own hands, suspended civil liberties during the Emergency (1975-1977), jailed opponents, imposed press censorship, and governed by decree. Voted out in 1977, she returned triumphantly in 1980 and adopted an increasingly hardline approach to regional insurgencies — in Punjab, in Kashmir, and in the northeast. By 1984, she was locked in a mortal struggle with a Sikh separatist movement that threatened to tear India apart. Her enemies called her a dictator. Her supporters called her Durga — the warrior goddess. She was, without question, the most powerful woman in the world.

"I don't mind if my life goes in the service of the nation. If I die today, every drop of my blood will invigorate the nation."

— Indira Gandhi, in her final public speech, Bhubaneswar, October 30, 1984 — the day before her assassination

⚔️ Operation Blue Star: The Desecration That Demanded Revenge

To understand why Indira Gandhi was killed, you must understand what happened in Amritsar in June 1984. In the early 1980s, Punjab was burning. A Sikh separatist movement, led by the charismatic and fiery preacher Jarnail Singh Bhindranwale, was demanding an independent Sikh state called Khalistan. Bhindranwale was originally promoted by Gandhi's son Sanjay to weaken Sikh political rivals — but he soon became uncontrollable. His followers carried out bombings, assassinations, and massacres across Punjab. By 1984, Bhindranwale and a heavily armed band of militants had fortified themselves inside the Golden Temple complex in Amritsar — the holiest shrine of the Sikh religion, a place of pilgrimage for millions. Gandhi faced an impossible choice: tolerate a terrorist stronghold in Sikhism's most sacred site, or violate the sanctity of the Golden Temple by sending in the army. She chose the military option.

🪖 June 1-8, 1984: Tanks at the Temple Gates

The Indian Army launched Operation Blue Star on June 1, 1984. Soldiers stormed the Golden Temple complex using tanks, artillery, and helicopter gunships. The fighting raged for five days. Bhindranwale was killed, along with hundreds of his militants. But so were over 500 pilgrims and civilians caught in the crossfire. The Akal Takht — the Sikh temporal authority, one of the five takhts (seats of power) of Sikhism — was severely damaged by tank fire. The Golden Temple's marble walls were pocked with bullet holes. The sacred pool was stained with blood. For Sikhs across the world, this was not a counter-terrorism operation. This was a sacrilege — a desecration of the holiest site in their religion. It was an unforgivable betrayal by a state they had served loyally for generations.

Operation Blue Star — June 1-8, 1984

"The army rolled into the Golden Temple at dawn. Tanks fired on the Akal Takht. The holiest shrine of our faith became a battlefield. Indira Gandhi had desecrated the house of our Guru. The entire Sikh community swore: she would pay in blood."

🔫 The Morning of October 31, 1984: 31 Bullets

After Operation Blue Star, intelligence agencies warned Gandhi repeatedly to remove Sikh members from her security detail. She refused. "Why should I?" she reportedly said. "They are my own people. I have no reason to distrust them." It was a fatal miscalculation — born either of genuine trust or of political calculation. Beant Singh, 33, had been a trusted member of Gandhi's security team for a decade. He had traveled with her internationally. He was a decorated officer. But he was also a deeply religious Sikh who had been radicalized by the desecration of the Golden Temple. Satwant Singh, 21, was a young recruit assigned to Gandhi's detail only five months before the assassination. On the morning of October 31, 1984, Gandhi was walking from her residence at 1 Safdarjung Road to her office in the adjoining bungalow, where she was scheduled to give an interview to Irish actor Peter Ustinov for a documentary. As she passed through the garden gate, Beant Singh stepped forward — he had abandoned his usual post. He fired three shots from his .38 service revolver. As Gandhi fell, Satwant Singh stepped forward with his Sten submachine gun and fired 25 rounds into her body. The two men threw down their weapons. Beant Singh was immediately shot by other members of the security detail and died on the spot. Satwant Singh was severely wounded but survived. Indira Gandhi, the most powerful woman in the world, lay bleeding on the gravel path where she had walked every morning. She never regained consciousness.

🔥 The Anti-Sikh Pogroms: Four Days of Hell

The news of Gandhi's assassination spread across India like wildfire. Within hours, organized mobs — many led by Congress Party politicians — began attacking Sikh communities across northern India. The violence was not spontaneous; it was orchestrated. Mobs were provided with voter lists identifying Sikh households. Sikh-owned businesses were looted and burned. Sikh men were dragged from their homes, doused in kerosene, and set on fire. Sikh women were raped. Sikh children were killed. The Delhi Police, under Congress Party control, largely stood by — or actively participated. For four days, the pogroms continued with impunity. By the time the violence subsided, an estimated 3,000 to 8,000 Sikhs had been killed. Over 50,000 were displaced. Rajiv Gandhi, sworn in as Prime Minister hours after his mother's death, infamously said: "When a big tree falls, the earth shakes." It was interpreted by many as a justification for the massacres. The anti-Sikh pogroms of 1984 remain one of the darkest chapters in independent India's history — a state-sanctioned massacre of a minority community, fueled by political opportunism and religious hatred. For decades, the perpetrators were protected. It took over 30 years for some low-level perpetrators to be convicted. The political masterminds were never held accountable.

📖 The Legacy: An Unhealed Wound

Indira Gandhi's assassination ended the life of a woman who, for all her authoritarian flaws, had been a towering figure in Indian politics. She had defeated Pakistan in the 1971 war and created Bangladesh. She had made India a nuclear power. She had stood up to the United States and aligned with the Soviet Union during the Cold War. Her death brought her son Rajiv to power — a reluctant politician who would himself be assassinated by a Tamil Tiger suicide bomber in 1991. The anti-Sikh pogroms created a wound in India's secular fabric that has never fully healed. The Sikh community — one of India's most successful and patriotic minorities — carries the memory of 1984 as a deep, unhealed trauma. The demand for justice — for the prosecution of the Congress politicians who organized the massacres — remains a live political issue in India to this day. Indira Gandhi's assassination was not just the murder of a prime minister. It was the act that set in motion a cycle of violence, revenge, and communal hatred whose echoes still reverberate through the subcontinent. 31 bullets. Thousands of dead. A wound that still bleeds.
