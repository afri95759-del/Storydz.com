ZERO

That's how many weapons of mass destruction were found in Iraq. Zero. None. The entire war — 200,000 dead, trillions of dollars spent, a nation destroyed — was built on a lie.

Colin Powell held up a vial at the UN. Bush said "we know where they are." They were wrong. And the Middle East is still burning.

On the night of March 20, 2003, the sky over Baghdad turned to fire. Anti-aircraft tracers streaked upward in desperate arcs. Explosions bloomed across the city — orange, white, red — as cruise missiles and precision bombs found their targets. It was the opening of "Shock and Awe" — the American military doctrine designed to paralyze an enemy with overwhelming, instantaneous force. In Baghdad, the ground shook. Windows shattered for miles. The power grid failed. The ancient city of the Abbasid caliphs — the city of Harun al-Rashid, of the Arabian Nights, of 5,000 years of civilization — went dark. President George W. Bush addressed the world: "At this hour, American and coalition forces are in the early stages of military operations to disarm Iraq, to free its people, and to defend the world from grave danger." The grave danger was Saddam Hussein's weapons of mass destruction. The intelligence was definitive, we were told. Colin Powell had shown the evidence to the United Nations — satellite photos, intercepted conversations, diagrams of mobile weapons labs. "These are facts, not assertions," he said. The world watched. The bombs fell. Baghdad burned. And in just 21 days, the regime of Saddam Hussein — the Butcher of Baghdad, the man who had ruled Iraq with an iron fist for 24 years — collapsed. On April 9, a statue of Saddam was pulled down in Firdos Square by U.S. Marines and jubilant Iraqis. On May 1, President Bush landed on the USS Abraham Lincoln in a flight suit, striding across the deck under a banner that read "MISSION ACCOMPLISHED." It was one of the most catastrophic misjudgments in the history of American foreign policy. The mission was not accomplished. It had barely begun. The invasion — swift, decisive, devastating — gave way to an occupation that unleashed 8 years of insurgency, civil war, and chaos. The weapons of mass destruction? They never existed. Iraq had destroyed its programs years earlier. The intelligence was wrong — catastrophically, systematically wrong. And the war that was sold to the world as a war of necessity became the most disastrous American military venture since Vietnam.

Summary: The 2003 Iraq War began on March 20, 2003, with the U.S.-led "Shock and Awe" bombing campaign. A coalition of American, British, and allied forces invaded Iraq with the stated goal of eliminating Saddam Hussein's weapons of mass destruction. Baghdad fell on April 9, 2003 — just 21 days after the invasion began. Saddam was captured on December 13, 2003, hiding in a "spider hole" near Tikrit. He was executed on December 30, 2006. However, the war devolved into a brutal insurgency (2003-2007) and sectarian civil war (2006-2007). No WMDs were ever found. Two catastrophic decisions — disbanding the Iraqi army and de-Baathification — fueled the insurgency. The Abu Ghraib torture scandal (2004) shattered America's moral standing. The U.S. "Surge" (2007-2008) reduced violence but failed to resolve underlying political conflicts. U.S. troops withdrew in December 2011. The war created a power vacuum that contributed directly to the rise of ISIS in 2014. Estimated deaths: 200,000+ Iraqis, 4,500+ Americans. Estimated cost: $2+ trillion.

📁 The Road to War: How a Lie Became a War

After the September 11, 2001 attacks, the Bush administration began building a case for war against Iraq — a country that had nothing to do with 9/11. Saddam Hussein and al-Qaeda were bitter enemies. But the administration insisted there were links. Vice President Dick Cheney, Defense Secretary Donald Rumsfeld, and Deputy Defense Secretary Paul Wolfowitz pushed relentlessly for regime change. On February 5, 2003, Secretary of State Colin Powell — a man of immense credibility, a former general, a figure respected across party lines — went before the United Nations Security Council. In a 75-minute presentation, he displayed satellite photographs, played intercepted phone calls, and held up a small vial to illustrate the potency of anthrax. "Every statement I make today is backed up by sources, solid sources," Powell said. "These are not assertions. What we're giving you are facts and conclusions based on solid intelligence." The presentation was devastating. The world watched. The UN was convinced. But the intelligence was a house of cards. The "mobile weapons labs" were milk trucks. The "aluminum tubes for centrifuges" were for conventional rockets. The "yellowcake uranium from Niger" was based on forged documents. Hans Blix, the chief UN weapons inspector, had found nothing. He pleaded for more time. The U.S. refused. On March 20, without UN authorization, with France, Germany, and Russia opposed, the bombs began falling on Baghdad.

⚡ "Shock and Awe": 21 Days That Crushed a Regime

The invasion was a display of overwhelming American military power. 150,000 American troops and 45,000 British troops surged north from Kuwait. Apache attack helicopters and A-10 Warthogs shredded Iraqi armored columns. Stealth bombers struck Baghdad with impunity. The Iraqi army — once the fourth largest in the world — melted away. Many soldiers simply took off their uniforms and walked home. On April 5, 2003, American tanks raced through the streets of Baghdad in the first "Thunder Run" — a reconnaissance mission that turned into a bold demonstration of dominance. Iraqi defenders fired RPGs from rooftops. American tanks fired back, obliterating buildings. The message was clear: Baghdad is ours. On April 9, Marines arrived at Firdos Square in central Baghdad. A crowd of Iraqis had gathered. With the help of an American armored recovery vehicle, they pulled down the massive bronze statue of Saddam Hussein — the dictator who had ruled them for a generation. An Iraqi man beat the statue's face with his shoes — the ultimate Arab insult. The image was broadcast live around the world. The regime had fallen. Cost of the invasion: 139 American soldiers killed. Iraqi military deaths: estimated 10,000–15,000. But the easy military victory was an illusion. The real war was about to begin.

🕳️ The Catastrophic Mistakes: How America Lost the Peace

The occupation of Iraq was managed by the Coalition Provisional Authority (CPA) under L. Paul Bremer, a former diplomat with no experience in post-conflict reconstruction. Bremer made two catastrophic decisions that turned a difficult occupation into an impossible one. First: De-Baathification. All members of Saddam's Baath Party — tens of thousands of schoolteachers, civil servants, engineers, and administrators — were fired overnight. Iraq's entire government apparatus collapsed. The people who knew how to run the country were now unemployed — and angry. Second: The Dissolution of the Iraqi Army. 400,000 Iraqi soldiers were suddenly jobless. They were armed. They were trained. And they were humiliated. Many of them — Sunni Arabs who had formed the backbone of Saddam's military — joined the growing insurgency. "When you have 400,000 men with guns and nothing to do," one analyst observed, "you have an insurgency." By 2004, Iraq was burning. The city of Fallujah, in the Sunni Triangle, became the heart of the rebellion. In April 2004, four Blackwater contractors were ambushed, killed, and their bodies burned and hung from a bridge — images that shocked America. The U.S. launched two brutal offensives to retake Fallujah. The second, in November 2004 — Operation Phantom Fury — was the bloodiest urban combat for American forces since the Vietnam War. House-to-house fighting. Snipers. IEDs. Fallujah was reduced to rubble. But the insurgency grew stronger.

📸 Abu Ghraib: The Photographs That Shamed a Superpower

In April 2004, the CBS program 60 Minutes II broadcast photographs that horrified the world. American soldiers — men and women in uniform — were shown torturing and humiliating Iraqi prisoners at Abu Ghraib prison. The same prison where Saddam Hussein had tortured his enemies. Now it was the Americans doing the torturing. A hooded prisoner standing on a box with wires attached to his fingers, told he would be electrocuted if he fell. A pyramid of naked, hooded men. A female soldier, Lynndie England, holding a leash attached to a naked prisoner's neck — like a dog. Smiling. Giving a thumbs-up. The photographs spread across the Arab world like wildfire. They became recruiting tools for al-Qaeda. They destroyed whatever moral credibility the American occupation had possessed. Investigations found the abuses were not isolated — they were systemic, the result of policies that blurred the lines between interrogation and torture. Several low-level soldiers were convicted. No senior official was ever held accountable. The photographs of Abu Ghraib remain, to this day, a permanent stain on America's reputation in the Middle East.

🔍 The Spider Hole: Finding Saddam

After Baghdad fell, Saddam Hussein vanished. For eight months, he was a ghost — releasing audio tapes urging Iraqis to resist the occupiers. The Americans placed a $25 million bounty on his head. On December 13, 2003, acting on a tip from a captured bodyguard, soldiers from the 4th Infantry Division surrounded a small farm near Tikrit, Saddam's hometown. They searched the compound. Nothing. Then someone noticed a rug on the ground. Under the rug: a Styrofoam panel. Under the panel: a hole. And inside the hole — a tiny underground chamber, just 2 meters long and 1 meter wide — was Saddam Hussein. Disheveled. Bearded. Broken. He had a pistol but didn't use it. "I am Saddam Hussein, the President of Iraq," he said in English. "I am willing to negotiate." An American soldier replied: "President Bush sends his regards." The Butcher of Baghdad — the man who had ruled Iraq for 24 years, who had started two wars, who had killed hundreds of thousands of his own people — was pulled from a hole in the ground like an animal. He was tried by an Iraqi court for crimes against humanity. On December 30, 2006, he was hanged. A cell phone video of his execution — showing him being taunted by Shia guards — was leaked. The man was dead. The chaos he left behind was far from over.

🕊️ The Surge, the Withdrawal, and the Rise of ISIS

By 2006, Iraq was in a full-blown sectarian civil war. Sunni and Shia militias were slaughtering each other. Death squads roamed the streets. Bodies were dumped in the Tigris River every morning. In 2007, President Bush ordered a "surge" — 30,000 additional troops, bringing the total to over 160,000. Under General David Petraeus, the U.S. implemented a new strategy: protect civilians, separate warring communities, and — critically — bribe Sunni tribal leaders to fight against al-Qaeda in what became known as the "Anbar Awakening." The surge reduced violence dramatically. But it was a tactical success that masked a strategic failure. The fundamental political divisions between Iraq's Shia majority and Sunni minority were never resolved. In 2008, the U.S. signed an agreement requiring all American troops to leave by the end of 2011. On December 18, 2011, the last U.S. military convoy crossed into Kuwait. The Iraq War was officially over. And three years later, the Islamic State — ISIS — rose from the ashes of the war.

MISSION ACCOMPLISHED? The Legacy of the Iraq War

"The Iraq War was sold with the promise of WMDs. None were found. It was sold as liberation. It became an occupation. It was supposed to bring democracy. It brought civil war. The war cost at least 200,000 Iraqi lives and 4,500 American lives. It cost over $2 trillion. It empowered Iran — Iraq's Shia-led government became closer to Tehran than to Washington. It created the conditions for ISIS. It shattered America's credibility. Twenty years later, Iraq is still broken. The question that haunts everyone — soldiers, diplomats, journalists, historians — is the simplest and the most terrible: Was it worth it? The answer — whispered by the ghosts of Baghdad, Fallujah, and the banks of the Tigris — is a silence that speaks louder than any words."

🤔 Frequently Asked Questions

1) Were WMDs ever found in Iraq? No. Zero. None. Despite years of searching by the Iraq Survey Group, no weapons of mass destruction were found. Iraq had destroyed its programs after the 1991 Gulf War.

2) How was Saddam Hussein captured? On December 13, 2003, he was found hiding in a tiny underground "spider hole" near Tikrit. He surrendered without a fight. He was executed on December 30, 2006.

3) What caused the insurgency? The CPA's decisions to disband the Iraqi army (400,000 armed men unemployed) and purge Baath Party members from government created the conditions for a massive insurgency.

4) Is Iraq stable today? Iraq remains fragile. Sectarian tensions, corruption, Iranian influence, and the legacy of ISIS continue to destabilize the country. The war's wounds are far from healed.
